# Methodology

How this was built, what standard of evidence it holds to, and what it deliberately does not do.

## The core principle

First-hand experience generated the hypotheses. Public research tested them.

I work on Morrisons Market Street in Glasgow. That is why the project exists, because it made me ask questions an outside analyst would not have thought to ask. It is also the main risk in the method, because one store is not a company. Everything below is built to keep those two things apart.

No observation from my store is presented as evidence of a Morrisons-wide pattern. Where public evidence backs an observation up, that is stated and sourced. Where it does not, the analysis says the observation would need validating across a wider sample of stores, and stops.

## Evidence typing

Every substantive claim carries one of six labels, and the deck shows them visually.

Public fact means credible external evidence, cited. First-hand observation means something I saw at one store. Hypothesis means it might be true and needs investigating. Inference means a conclusion drawn from the evidence available. Recommendation means what the analysis suggests Morrisons considers. Internal validation required means it cannot be established from outside the company.

That last label gets used freely rather than avoided. Twelve questions material to this analysis have no public answer, and naming them precisely is worth more than estimating around them.

## Sourcing

Sources are prioritised in three tiers.

Tier 1 is company primary and official: Morrisons corporate press releases, results and trading statements, official product and sustainability pages, competitor corporate releases, and government or regulatory publications including the Food Standards Agency and House of Commons Library.

Tier 2 is business and trade press: The Grocer, Retail Gazette, Grocery Gazette, Retail Week, Reuters, Which?, Meat Management, ESM, City AM, Supermarket News, Grocery Dive.

Tier 3 is research, consultancy and vendor material: ECR Retail Loss, University of Portsmouth, WRAP, IGD, Lumina Intelligence, FMI, Retail Economics. Vendor case studies are used only to establish that a capability exists or that a named customer uses it, and every vendor performance claim is labelled as an unaudited vendor claim.

Some things were rejected on quality. SEO aggregator sites are excluded outright. Subway UK and Pret price points could not come from those, and neither operator publishes a UK price list, so the prices in the deck are operator-listed prices for named branches on named delivery platforms, on a stated date, labelled as such. That is a weaker source than an in-store price and the deck says so.

All 104 sources are in [`source-register.md`](source-register.md) with a note on what each was used for.

## What this deliberately does not do

No fabricated figures. No Morrisons procurement costs, margins, store profitability, waste rates, labour allocations, transaction volumes, internal KPIs or synthetic datasets appear anywhere.

No financial model. I built a unit economics model early in the work and left it out. Projecting a profit outcome for Morrisons from external price data would have meant assuming their input costs, their cannibalisation rate and their demand, which are the three things that decide the answer. The honest output was a break-even threshold and a list of what internal data would settle it.

No forced frameworks. No SWOT, PESTLE, Five Forces, Ansoff or BCG matrix. A prioritisation matrix appears because the deck genuinely needs to compare interventions on two dimensions, and its scores are presented as structured judgement rather than as objective ranking.

No national rollout recommended on external research. Every recommendation carries a three-store pilot with matched controls, a defined period, success measures written down before launch, and a stated decision point.

## The verification pass

Everything carried over from earlier working notes was re-verified against primary sources before it reached a slide. That found four errors worth catching.

The counter numbers. Seventy counters were closed in March 2025. Morrisons began a phased reinstatement in July 2026 and has not committed to reopening all of them. An earlier draft had the reinstatement figure wrong.

The £940m. Morrisons reports £942m of cumulative cost savings against a £1bn programme target, and its chief executive credits data and AI as a contributor to that total. It is not an AI savings figure, and presenting it as one misreads the primary source.

The loss figures. The £381m pre-tax loss sits at the trading entity for the year to 26 October 2025. Filed accounts reported in August 2026 show a larger statutory loss and higher net debt at the ultimate parent. Both are disclosed rather than whichever is more convenient.

Asda's food-to-go. Two separate initiatives four years apart, the 2020 conversion of counters to self-service hot stations and the 2023 front-of-store Food Hub concession, were being treated as one thing.

Where sources could not be reconciled, such as a vendor's "30% improvement in on-shelf availability" against contemporaneous reporting of "shelf gaps down 30%", the deck uses the more conservative reading and records the discrepancy.

## Confidentiality

The analysis names Morrisons throughout, as it has to. It also has to be publishable by someone who works there.

Excluded from anything public: named internal systems and applications, device provisioning detail, internal data capture, any observation framed as a compliance failure or food safety lapse, store-level performance or sales or waste or stock figures, individual colleagues, and anything sourced from an internal document or screen.

Retained at process-category level: that counter production decisions rely substantially on colleague judgement, that real-time visibility of bulk back-of-house stock is difficult to maintain, that customers buying deli meat sometimes volunteer that it is for a sandwich, and that reductions are done by judgement under time pressure.

Each of those is labelled as a first-hand observation, paired with whatever external evidence does or does not back it up, and never generalised to the company.

## Working process

Research finished before any slide was designed.

First, extract the observations, hypotheses, sources, assumptions and confidentiality risks from prior working notes. Second, research in three parallel streams: verifying the Morrisons narrative, benchmarking the UK food-to-go market and competitors, and mapping fresh-food waste, in-store operations and the technology landscape. Third, challenge everything, kill what does not survive, and look for opportunities the original thinking missed. Five candidate interventions were screened out at that stage because Morrisons already solves them. Fourth, synthesise into a problem statement, issue tree, opportunity landscape and recommendation set. Fifth, write a storyline where the slide titles alone carry the argument, and review it before building anything. Sixth, build, then check every slide for logic, evidence, title, visual, density, sourcing and confidentiality.

## Known limits

Single-store observation, not a multi-store study. No access to internal Morrisons data of any kind. UK food-to-go channel shares are paywalled, so the deck uses qualitative channel positioning rather than an invented split. Two published UK food-to-go market sizings differ by roughly £3bn because they measure different things, so one is quoted with its publisher and definition named and they are never blended. The peer-reviewed retail-loss research covers European retailers generally, not Morrisons. Vendor outcome claims are unaudited and labelled throughout. And no Subway in-store price could be sourced, which is the weakest link in the price ladder and is flagged on the slide that uses it.
